Abstract—Forecasting desertification has received far less at-
tention than mapping it. We use a convolutional LSTM (ConvL-
STM) trained on ten annual Earth Observation (EO) variables
(2000–2023) over the semi-arid state of Cear´a, Brazil, on a
256×206 grid at roughly 2.2 km resolution, to measure how
much of the year-to-year evolution of a MapBiomas-derived
degradation index (IDV) can be predicted from satellite data. The
network predicts a bounded change on top of the last observed
state, is trained in two stages (single-step, then recursive rollout)
and is evaluated on a contiguous spatial and temporal holdout.
State-wide mean IDV rose from 0.238 to 0.350 between 2000 and
2023, about 0.005 per year, but cell-level change is dominated
by year-to-year jumps, and a gradient-boosting model given all
channels explains only 4.7% of its variance. Against this ceiling,
the ConvLSTM lowers RMSE relative to persistence by 1.6% one
year ahead and 4.4% four years ahead in a held-out southern
band (p < 0.001), beats persistence with a constant drift and
reproduces 53–69% of the regional drift. Drift-aware baselines
only help from the third year onward, by up to 4.4% in RMSE
at six years. An earlier formulation that redrew the whole field
each year predicted changes unrelated to the observed ones
(correlation −0.06) and produced a spurious decline when run
recursively. Channel knockouts suggest that elevation and EVI
degrade the forecast, while slope and aspect have no measurable
effect. Monte Carlo dropout and stochastic scenario maps convey
the uncertainty that the expected-value forecast hides. Under
climatological forcing, mean IDV rises from 0.350 in 2023 to
0.370 in 2030. EO-based desertification forecasting is therefore
reliable for the regional trend, not for individual cells.
Index Terms—deep learning, desertification forecasting, earth
observation, predictability, persistence baseline, remote sensing
HIGHLIGHTS

• Less than 5% of next-year change at 2.2 km is predictable

I. INTRODUCTION
A sustainable world depends on environmental monitoring
systems able to read the present state of terrestrial ecosystems
accurately and to anticipate where those ecosystems are head-
ing under mounting climatic and anthropogenic pressure. Earth
Observation (EO) is one of the primary scientific infrastruc-
tures behind that effort, supplying measurements of the land
surface through satellite remote sensing [1]. EO archives have
grown over the last two decades, and together with advances in
artificial intelligence that growth has pushed the field toward
predictive environmental intelligence [2]. The most recent
work goes further. Large pretrained foundation models now
learn reusable geospatial representations from heterogeneous,
multimodal EO sources, placing multispectral imagery and
climatic products in a single learned representation [2], [3].
These models transfer more easily across remote sensing tasks
and reduce the cost of adapting to new ones, which has made
them a central computational framework for EO with clear
relevance to climate adaptation and the SDGs, particularly
SDG 13 (Climate Action) and SDG 15 (Life on Land) [1],
[4], [5].
Most of this progress has gone toward image interpretation
and land-cover classification. General-purpose forecasting, and
desertification forecasting in particular, remains comparatively
underexplored [2], [3], [5]. Forecasting asks a different ques-
tion from classification: which dynamical mechanisms govern
how the state evolves under climatic, ecological and anthro-
pogenic conditions that keep shifting [5]? It is a problem of
temporal reasoning under uncertainty, and the influence of
partially observed processes grows as the horizon lengthens
[5]. Methods that represent these dynamics, and that locate the
point at which partial observability starts to dominate forecast
error, are one of the open directions in Geospatial Artificial
Intelligence [2], [5].
The United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification
(UNCCD) defines desertification as land degradation in arid,
semi-arid and dry sub-humid regions resulting from climatic
variability and human activity [6]. Unlike most environmental
forecasting targets, it has no single directly observable variable

Left unchecked, these coupled processes erode ecosystem
resilience, with consequences for e.g., agricultural productiv-
ity, biodiversity, and more. The affected populations become
more vulnerable, and the losses extend to carbon storage and
socioeconomic stability, touching Zero Hunger (SDG 2), Clean
Water and Sanitation (SDG 6), Climate Action (SDG 13) and
Life on Land (SDG 15) [4], [6], [9].
Northeastern Brazil’s semi-arid region is a clear example.
Recurrent droughts and vegetation degradation reinforce each
other there and drive a steady deterioration of the landscape
[10], [11]. EO has done much to characterize this process as
it unfolded: land-cover products and each general information
contribute part of the historical picture [8], [11]. Forecast-
ing where it goes next is considerably harder, since many
of the mechanisms driving desertification are observed only
indirectly, if at all, or at resolutions that do not match the
rest of the record [7], [11]. A forecasting framework meant
to inform decisions therefore needs more than accuracy. It
needs a fair reference for what is predictable, some notion
of physical consistency, uncertainty quantification, and the
ability to absorb richer EO data as it becomes available [5],
[12]. These properties matter more as specialized forecasting
modules are folded into EO foundation-model ecosystems [3],
[5], [12].
Among deep learning architectures suited to this kind of
problem, the Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory net-
work (ConvLSTM) [13] is a natural starting point. Convo-
lutional operations are embedded inside its recurrent memory
cells, so it learns how spatiotemporal patterns evolve across a
multivariate EO time series while keeping the spatial structure
of the data [13], [14].
We study such a model in the Brazilian state of Cear´a,
a semi-arid testbed with pronounced climatic variability and
a well-documented history of desertification [9], [10]. The
ConvLSTM is used as an instrument to answer three questions.
How much of the year-to-year change in a satellite-derived
degradation index can be predicted from the EO record avail-
able today? At which horizon, if any, does a learned model
add information beyond the persistence of the current state?
And what does that limit say about the processes that EO
does and does not observe? Our contributions are (i) a model-
independent estimate of the predictability of annual degrada-
tion change in Cear´a, (ii) a residual ConvLSTM trained with a
curriculum and evaluated against that estimate on a contiguous
spatial and temporal holdout, and (iii) the diagnosis of a failure
mode in which a network that reconstructs the whole field
produces spurious mean reversion when run recursively.
II. METHODOLOGY
A. Study Area
The study covers the state of Cear´a in northeastern Brazil,
a semi-arid region with pronounced hydroclimatic variability
and extensive land degradation [15], [16]. It is one of the
country’s principal desertification-affected areas. Prolonged
droughts coexist with vegetation degradation and land-use
pressures such as burning and slash-and-burn agriculture, and
these interact across scales to leave a heterogeneous footprint
of degradation on the landscape [9], [10], [16], [17]. Cear´a
is a useful testbed because relatively preserved caatinga and
severely degraded terrain occur side by side, giving a wide
spread of environmental trajectories against which a forecast
can be tested [15], [16].
B. Earth Observation Dataset Construction
All variables were assembled on Google Earth Engine
(GEE) [18] through its Python API, aggregated to annual
resolution for 2000–2023 and exported onto a common grid
of 256 × 206 cells in EPSG:4326, covering 41.4°W–37.3°W
and 7.9°S–2.8°S with cells of about 0.02° (roughly 2.2 km).
Products with finer native resolution were first brought to 1 km
by a maximum-value reduction over the contributing pixels
followed by reprojection, and all layers were then resampled
to this grid.
Ten variables make up the dataset (Table I). The forecasting
target is the Integrated Desertification Vulnerability (IDV)
index, derived from the MapBiomas land use and land cover
collection [25]. Pixels classified as pasture or other non-
vegetated area are set to one and all others to zero, and
the resulting binary map is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel
(σ = 1 pixel). After aggregation to the analysis grid, the IDV
of a cell is a continuous value in [0, 1] that expresses the
presence and intensity of degradation-prone cover within it.
The other nine variables are predictors. Climate and hydrology
are described by annual precipitation from CHIRPS [19],
mean daytime land surface temperature (LST) from MODIS
MOD11A2 [20], and the Standardized Precipitation Index on
a 12-month accumulation (SPI-12) [24], obtained from the
annual CHIRPS totals by fitting a gamma distribution per
pixel. Vegetation is described by NDVI and EVI from MODIS
MOD13Q1 [21]. Soil moisture is the annual mean volumetric
water content of the second ERA5-Land layer (7–28 cm)
[23]. Because the annual mean was sampled without a fixed
projection, this channel was extracted at about 1° and carries
only the broad regional pattern of soil moisture. Elevation

C. Normalization
Each channel is mapped to [0, 1] using the 2nd and 98th
percentiles of its valid cells over the training years (2000–
2017), and values outside that range are clipped. Restricting
the percentiles to valid cells matters here. When the fill values
outside the state were included, the lower percentile of NDVI
was set by the fill value (0.30, against 0.39 inside the state),
precipitation lost the lower 312 mm of its range, and the upper
percentile of IDV dropped to 0.967, which clipped 3.5% of
the valid cells in the training years. Those clipped cells are
the most degraded ones, so the old statistics distorted exactly
the part of the target that matters most.

regime. It also avoids a subtle problem of carrying the state

autoregressive, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation
rollout loss is kept.
G. Recursive Inference
The 2024–2030 projection starts from the observed window
2021–2023. At each step the model forecasts the IDV of the
next year, and a new frame is assembled from that forecast
and the exogenous channels. Static channels (elevation, slope,
aspect) keep their observed values. The dynamic exogenous
channels (precipitation, LST, NDVI, EVI, soil moisture and
SPI-12) are set to their per-cell 2000–2023 mean in normalized
units. The new frame replaces the oldest one in the window
and the process repeats. Cells outside the validity mask are
held at their observed value, so the model’s output there is
never fed back into the window.
The projection is therefore conditional on average climate. It
describes how the degradation pattern would evolve if future
years resembled the climatological mean, and it should not
be read as a climate prediction. Section III-D shows that this
choice has a small effect on the hindcast.
H. Uncertainty Quantification
We use Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout [31]. The spatial
dropout layers of the selected network are kept active at
inference, and D = 50 stochastic recursive rollouts give
a mean ¯YMC and a per-cell standard deviation σMC. MC
Dropout approximates the variability of one network around
its learned weights, so it should be read as an indicator of
relative confidence across space and time, not as a calibrated
probability. The 95% bands are ¯YMC ± 1.96 σMC, clipped
to [0, 1]. The cell-level variability that this spread does not
capture is represented by the scenarios of Section II-I.
I. Scenario Maps
The network forecasts the expected IDV of each cell, and the
expected value of a process that changes by jumps is smooth.
In the observed record about 55% of the valid cells do not
change at all from one year to the next, while the rest move by
amounts with a standard deviation of about 0.05. The expected
forecast instead moves every cell by a few thousandths per

5
year. Over several recursive steps this keeps the regional mean
on track but pulls the cells at both ends of the scale toward
the middle, so the maps lose the stable cells at zero and the
saturated hotspots that the observed maps keep.
To obtain maps with realistic cell-level behaviour we gener-
ate stochastic scenarios around the network forecast. At each
step and cell, the change is either zero, with the probability pb
observed for cells of the same IDV level b (eleven classes
from 0 to 1), or a jump drawn from the observed non-
zero changes of that class. The jumps Jb are shifted by
an amount s chosen so that the expected change of every
cell, (1 −pb) (E[Jb] + s), equals the change µ forecast by
the network, which keeps the scenario mean anchored to the
ConvLSTM. Two latent Gaussian fields drive the draws, one
deciding whether a cell changes and one setting the size of
the jump. Both are smoothed with a Gaussian kernel whose
width reproduces the observed spatial correlation of stable
cells, and the first follows a first-order autoregression in time
whose coefficient reproduces the observed probability that a
cell stays stable two years in a row. Each scenario is fed back
into the network, so the network sees its own sampled history.
The class probabilities and jump distributions are estimated
from observed changes only, using the training years for the
hindcast and all years for the projection. We run 40 scenarios.
J. Predictability Reference
To judge the network fairly we first measure how predictable
the target is, using methods that do not depend on the
ConvLSTM.
Transition statistics. For all pairs of consecutive years in
the training period we compute the annual change ∆Yt+1 =
Yt+1 −Yt at every valid cell and summarize it by the level of
Yt. The mean of ∆Y in each class is what an ideal predictor
that knows only the current level would forecast.
Simple baselines. Starting from the observed 2017 map, we
forecast 2018–2023 with (i) persistence, (ii) persistence plus
the training-period mean annual change applied k times, (iii)
persistence plus the mean change of the current IDV class,
iterated year by year, and (iv) a per-cell linear trend fitted to
2000–2017. These baselines are evaluated over all valid cells
and over the southern validation band.
Learned upper reference. A gradient-boosting regressor [36]
(300 iterations, learning rate 0.05, at most 63 leaves, at least
200 samples per leaf) is trained to predict ∆Yt+1 at each cell.
Its features are Yt, the two most recent annual changes, the
mean of Yt over 3 × 3, 9 × 9 and 21 × 21 neighbourhoods,
the 9 × 9 mean of the most recent change, the values at t
and t + 1 and their difference for each dynamic exogenous
channel, and elevation and slope. It is trained on 2002–2017
transitions in the northern training block and tested on 2018–
2023 transitions with observed inputs. This reference sees
the observed exogenous values of the target year, as the
ConvLSTM does during validation, so it is a generous estimate
of what the ten channels can explain.
Change skill. RMSE, R2 and SSIM between forecast and
observed maps are dominated by the spatial pattern, which
persistence reproduces almost perfectly. To measure whether
a model predicts change, we compare its one-step predicted
change ˆ∆t+1 = ˆYt+1 −Yt with the observed change through
their correlation and their standard deviations. We also scale
the predicted change by a factor α ∈[0, 1] and find the α that
minimizes RMSE on training years and rows. A value near
one means the predicted changes are informative at their full
size, and a value near zero means they only add error.

coefficient of determination R2, the Pearson correlation r and

a strict holdout. Because the validation band lies in the south,
the in-zone patches of Sul Seco and Cariri come entirely from
the validation band, and their gap partly reflects the train–
validation split itself.
III. RESULTS
A. Observed Dynamics and Predictability
Mean IDV over valid cells rose from 0.238 in 2000 to 0.318
in 2017 and 0.350 in 2023, an average of +0.0047 per year
over the training period and +0.0055 per year over 2018–
2023. The rise is not uniform. In the northern training block
mean IDV went from 0.190 to 0.257 between 2000 and 2023
(+0.0024 per year until 2017 and +0.0045 afterwards). In the
southern validation band it went from 0.257 to 0.401 (+0.0054
and +0.0088 per year). The validation band therefore degrades
faster than the region the model learns from, and faster in the
validation years than in the training years.

C. Does the Network Predict Change?
An earlier version of the model used a sigmoid output
layer, so that it had to reproduce the whole IDV field every


PREPRINT 2026
8
year, and carried its recurrent state from one forecast step to
the next. Over the four-year validation rollout it reached R2
and SSIM values of about 0.88, which look reasonable for a
multi-year forecast. Its one-step predicted changes, however,
were unrelated to the observed ones. The correlation between
predicted and observed annual change was −0.03 over 2003–
2017 and −0.06 over 2018–2022. When the predicted change
was scaled by a factor α, the RMSE on training years and
rows was lowest at α = 0, which means the best use of its
predictions was to discard the change and keep persistence.
Its errors were small relative to the field but about as large
as the real annual changes (standard deviation of about 0.04
against 0.05), and they were not random in their effect. Run
recursively, they pulled intermediate and high IDV values
downward. In a 2018–2023 hindcast with observed climate,
the model’s mean IDV fell by 0.006 while the observed mean
rose by 0.033. Its 2024–2030 projection dropped by 0.014 in
the first year and declined again after 2026, a trajectory that
has no support in the record.
The residual formulation of Eq. (8) was introduced to
remove this failure mode, since any change the network
outputs must now be learned explicitly. For the residual
ConvLSTM, the correlation between predicted and observed
one-step change is about 0.13 on training years and rows, and
it stays at that level on the validation years, both over the
state and in the southern band. For comparison, the R2 of
the gradient-boosting reference corresponds to a correlation
of about 0.22 over the state and 0.11 in the southern band.
The predicted changes are small, with a standard deviation
of 0.004–0.005 against 0.052 observed and 0.014 for the
gradient-boosting reference, so the network commits only to
the part of the change it can support. The RMSE-minimizing
scale factor on training data is α = 1, the largest value tested,
which means its predicted changes are worth keeping in full
and are not inflated. Over the validation rollout, the model
reproduces 66%, 69%, 62% and 53% of the observed drift in
mean IDV at k = 1 to 4, always with the correct sign. The
CNN, in contrast, captures the drift at k = 1 and loses it with
each recursive step, reaching −22% at k = 4. The network
has therefore learned a small but real part of the predictable
change, consistent in training and validation years, and it
under-predicts the drift in the south, as the drift baselines do,
because that region degraded faster than the training region.

H. Physical Consistency
The projection passes all five checks. Four of them are
computed over the observed and projected series together,
2000–2030, and none of them shows a break at the transition
from observation to projection. Over that period the degraded-
area fraction changes by at most 1.2 percentage points from
one year to the next (limit 15), the hotspots of consecutive
years overlap with a mean IoU of 0.91 (limit 0.2), the centroid
of the degraded area moves by at most 1.2 cells (limit 30), and
the lag-one autocorrelation of the mean IDV is 0.997 (limit
0.4). The fifth check looks at the projection alone. Its largest
annual expansion of the degraded area is 0.25 percentage
points, a fifth of the largest observed between 2000 and 2023
(1.2 points). As noted in Section II-K, these checks only
show that the projection does not move or inflate degradation
implausibly. A model close to persistence passes them almost
by construction, so they cannot distinguish a good forecast
from a cautious one.
I. Uncertainty
Table VII and Fig. 10 summarize the MC Dropout spread,
which grows steadily with lead time, from 0.0008 in 2024 to
0.0021 in 2030, as the stochastic rollouts drift apart. Averaged
over the state, the 95% band in 2030 runs from 0.366 to 0.374,
entirely above the 2023 value, so the sign of the projected
change is robust to the model’s own uncertainty. These spreads
are about 25 times smaller than the year-to-year change of a
typical cell. It is a spread of the model’s own predictions and
does not account for the cell-level variability that Section III-A
shows to be unpredictable, which has a standard deviation of
about 0.05 per year. A forecast for a single cell is uncertain
by at least that amount whatever the reported spread, and
the reported spreads are better read as the uncertainty of the
projected regional trend.
IV. DISCUSSION
Desertification is usually described as a cumulative pro-
cess, the product of long-term interactions among vegetation
degradation, climatic variability, hydrological processes, land
surface conditions, and other environmental factors.[7], [11],
[26], [30]. Our results agree with that description at the scale of
the region and disagree with any expectation of predictability
at the scale of the pixel. The degradation index in Cear´a has
risen steadily for 24 years, and that rise is visible and partly
forecastable. What happens to an individual 2.2 km cell in a
given year is mostly not. The median annual change is zero
in every IDV class, the typical change is ten times larger than

Part of this unpredictability comes from the target. The
IDV is derived from annual land-cover maps, and year-to-
year reclassification between pasture, other non-vegetated land
and natural cover produces jumps that are partly real land-use
change and partly classification noise. Neither is well predicted
by the climate and vegetation of the preceding years. The other
part comes from what EO can see. Every variable used here
describes the surface. None directly observes groundwater, soil
processes, land tenure, grazing pressure or the economic deci-
sions that lead a farmer to clear or abandon a plot. The satellite
record captures the surface expression of degradation, and the
gap between that expression and its causes limits forecasting
no matter which architecture is used. The gradient-boosting
reference turns this argument into a number for Cear´a: with
the information in these ten products, the predictable share of
annual cell-level change is close to 5%.
This has a direct consequence for how forecasting models
should be evaluated. Persistence reaches R2 and SSIM values
close to one in this problem because the spatial pattern of
degradation changes slowly. Any model that copies the last
map scores almost as well, so high values of these metrics
say little about whether a model has learned dynamics. The
earlier sigmoid model is a clear example. Its maps looked
right and its scores were close to those of persistence, but its
predicted changes were uncorrelated with the observed ones,
and when its output was fed back over seven years those errors
accumulated into a decline in degradation that the data do not
support. Errors in a recursive forecast do not cancel. They are
fed back into the input, and when they depend on the state, as
reconstruction errors near the bounds of [0, 1] do, they push the
whole field in one direction. A residual output that starts from
persistence makes the learned part of the forecast explicit, and
correlating that part with the observed change is a direct test
of skill. We think this test, together with drift-aware baselines,
should be standard when deep learning is applied to persistent
EO fields.
The regional pattern of the drift also matters. The south
of the state degraded about twice as fast as the north over
2000–2023, and both regions accelerated after 2017. A model
trained on the north and tested on the south is therefore asked
to extrapolate to faster change than it has seen, and even a
correct drift estimate from the training region falls short there,
as the drift baselines show. Similar spatial heterogeneity in the
rate of degradation has been reported across drylands, where
degradation tends to persist and intensify in places already
under pressure [26], [29], [30]. The channel knockouts point in
the same direction. No channel changes the error by more than
1%. Land surface temperature is the one the network relies on,
which fits its role as a direct measure of the surface energy
balance and of vegetation water stress. The vegetation indices,
often the most informative observable proxies of degradation
because they integrate water availability, thermal stress and
land management [27], [28], and elevation slightly degrade the
forecast in the validation band. Much of what the vegetation
indices carry about the state of the land is already in the last
IDV map, which the residual model uses directly, and what
the network learned from them and from elevation in the north
appears to be specific to the training region.
The practical message is that EO-based forecasting of de-
sertification is most useful at the scale where it is predictable.
The regional trend, and its uncertainty, can be projected with
some confidence from the record. Maps of individual cells
are mostly the present pattern carried forward, and presenting

V. LIMITATIONS AND ONGOING WORK
All results come from forecasting under partial observ-
ability. Groundwater, soil degradation, land management and
socioeconomic pressures are at best indirectly observed in
the products used here, and our predictability estimate is
conditional on these ten variables at 2.2 km and annual
resolution. Richer inputs could raise it.
The record is short. Twenty-four years limit both the training
sample and the statistical power of every comparison. The
validation set is also small: 18 patch-windows from a single
band in the far south of the state, which makes the validation
metrics sensitive to the particular trajectory of that region in
2018–2023. The 64 rows between the training and validation
blocks, about a quarter of the grid, are not used at all. A
blocked cross-validation over several bands, with the middle
of the state included, would give more stable estimates.
The zone-stratified evaluation does not retrain the model
with zones withheld, and the channel knockout does not retrain
without the removed channels. Both measure properties of one
trained network. A strict zone holdout and retrained ablations
would answer the questions of transfer and information content
more directly.
Some data choices affect the meaning of the variables. All
channels were aggregated to 1 km with a maximum-value
reduction. For the IDV this makes a cell degraded if any
part of it is, and for continuous variables such as NDVI and
LST it biases values upward. A mean reduction for continuous
channels should be compared. SPI-12 was standardized with
a gamma fit over the full 2000–2023 record, so it contains
a small amount of information from the validation years.
MapBiomas classification noise is part of the target, and
separating it from real land-cover change would sharpen the
predictability estimate.
The 2024–2030 projection uses climatological forcing. It
does not account for future droughts, El Ni˜no events or
changes in land use, and its MC Dropout bands reflect model
spread, not the cell-level variability described in Section III-A.
The scenarios add that variability, but they assume that the
jump statistics of 2000–2023 continue unchanged.
The benchmark is limited to persistence, drift baselines, a
CNN, an LSTM and the ConvLSTM. Given how little of the
change is predictable, we do not expect larger architectures
alone to help much. Better targets and better inputs seem more
promising: forecasting the IDV aggregated to coarser units
or regions, probabilistic models of transitions between land-
cover classes, explicit treatment of the regional drift, additional
observations related to land use and water, and representations
from multimodal EO foundation models.
Cear´a was chosen as a representative semi-arid testbed.
Whether the same limits apply in other drylands is still
open, and repeating the analysis elsewhere would separate
region-specific dynamics from limits that come from partial
observability in general.
Several of these points are being addressed in the version
of this work under preparation. First, we are retraining the
network without the channels that degraded the knockout
results (elevation and EVI), and with the other terrain and

of each group is measured on models trained without it and
not only on one trained network. Second, we are replacing the
single northern/southern split with a blocked cross-validation
over several latitudinal bands, including the middle of the state,
which should also supply the strict zone holdout missing here.
Third, we are widening the grid of shrinkage factors applied
to the predicted change, since the optimum reached the upper
edge of the current grid, and comparing mean and maximum

is replaced by wet and dry sequences, including El Ni˜no and
La Ni˜na years, to test how much of the regional drift depends
on the climate state. Finally, we are looking for additional data,
both longer and finer records for Cear´a and datasets from other
semi-arid regions, so that the same predictability analysis can
be applied to other drylands and show whether the 5% ceiling
is specific to Cear´a.
VI. CONCLUSION
We used a ConvLSTM trained on ten EO variables over
2000–2023 to probe how predictable desertification dynamics
are in Cear´a. Measured directly from the data, the mean
degradation index rose by about 0.005 per year, faster in the
south, while the year-to-year change of individual 2.2 km
cells was mostly unpredictable. A flexible model given every
channel explained about 5% of it, and no simple predictor
improved on persistence one year ahead at the state scale. A
residual ConvLSTM that starts from persistence and learns
only the change improved on persistence by about 1–2% one
year ahead, in every validation year, and by about 4% four
years ahead, and it reproduced a little over half of the regional
drift. That is close to the ceiling set by the data in the south of
the state and well short of what a perfect forecast of individual
cells would need. An earlier model that redrew the whole
field each year matched persistence on standard metrics while
predicting changes unrelated to the observed ones, and its
recursive projection showed a spurious decline in degradation.
Under climatological forcing, the projection for 2024–2030
continues the rise of the mean IDV, from 0.350 to 0.370, at
about half the recent rate, so it is more likely to underestimate
degradation in 2030 than to overstate it.
The main conclusion is that, with current EO products,
desertification can be forecast as a regional trend more than as
a map of where degradation will appear next. Evaluating deep
learning models against persistence, drift baselines and the
correlation of predicted and observed change makes this limit
visible, and the same analysis can be applied to any dryland
covered by global EO archives.
