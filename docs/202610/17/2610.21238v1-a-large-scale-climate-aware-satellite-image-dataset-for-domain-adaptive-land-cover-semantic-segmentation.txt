Abstract
A few well-annotated datasets for land-cover semantic segmentation have recently been introduced to advance the field of
earth observation technologies. However, these datasets overlook the significant diversity among geographic areas with different
climates, which can greatly impact and diversify land cover. Consequently, this leads to a domain gap in remote sensing images
and severe performance degradation of the segmentation models. To enhance land-cover semantic segmentation with improved
generalization ability, we conducted the first investigation into the impact of climate on this task. In this paper, we present
a unique large-scale Climate-Aware Satellite Images Dataset (CASID) specifically designed for domain adaptive land-cover
semantic segmentation. It consists of 980 satellite images with a size of 5,000 × 5,000 pixels, collected from 30 different regions
around Asia, covering over 24,500 square kilometers. These images are gathered from four distinct climate zones, namely
temperate monsoon, subtropical monsoon, tropical monsoon, and tropical rainforest. It includes four sub-datasets/domains, each
representing one of the aforementioned climate zones. This characteristic makes CASID the first climate-aware land-cover semantic
segmentation dataset with multiple domains. Additionally, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the samples from the four
climate zones, emphasizing differences in global image features, image texture, category distribution, spectral value, and object
shape. These analyses offer valuable insights for subsequent research in this field. Moreover, we conduct extensive experiments
to evaluate the latest semantic segmentation and unsupervised domain adaptation methods on the CASID dataset. These results
serve as a robust baseline for future research endeavors. Our dataset will be made publicly available soon at the following link:
https://github.com/Linwei-Chen/CASID.
Keywords: satellite image, semantic segmentation, unsupervised domain adaptation.
1. Introduction
Land-cover semantic segmentation, which aims to classify the
land-cover type for each pixel in remote sensing images, plays
a crucial role in enhancing our understanding of geographical
and ecological environments (Ma et al., 2021, Tong et al., 2020).
It finds widespread applications in various remote sensing do-
mains, such as urban planning (Dhanaraj and Angadi, 2021),
precision agriculture (do Nascimento Bendini et al., 2019), food
insecurity analysis (Olsen et al., 2021), and environmental pro-
tection (Bosquilia and Muller-Karger, 2021).
Existing mainstream semantic segmentation methods for re-
mote sensing images (Chen et al., 2018, Zhao et al., 2017, Long
et al., 2015, Ma et al., 2021, Chai et al., 2020, Wang et al.,
2022, Yang et al., 2021) are predominantly based on deep learn-
ing, which is a data-driven technology. These methods heavily
rely on the quality and quantity of the dataset to achieve high
accuracy. Consequently, datasets play a crucial role in the ad-
vancement of land-cover semantic segmentation for earth obser-
vation. To foster progress in this field, several remote sensing
datasets with well-annotated pixel-wise labels for land-cover
semantic segmentation have been recently introduced, such as
the Zurich Summer dataset (Volpi and Ferrari, 2015), Deep-
Globe dataset (Demir et al., 2018), and Gaofen Image Dataset
(GID) (Tong et al., 2020).
However, these datasets are primarily designed for standard
land-cover semantic segmentation and fail to consider the signif-
icant diversity among different geographic areas. This oversight
leads to a domain gap and can cause severe degradation of deep
learning models (Wang et al., 2021a). In particular, land cover
is profoundly influenced and diversified by climate factors (Dale,
1997, Reidmiller et al., 2019). The climate serves as a major de-
terminant of vegetation distribution (Prentice, 1990, Woodward
et al., 2004, Salazar et al., 2007). For instance, areas within the
tropical rainforest climate zone are typically covered by dense
rainforests, whereas the temperate monsoon climate zone of-
ten exhibits bare ground surfaces with fewer trees, owing to
differences in temperature and precipitation. Therefore, the dis-
tribution and pixel spectra of the forest category in the temperate
monsoon climate zone can differ significantly from those in the
tropical rainforest climate zone.
Moreover, both climate and vegetation have a significant im-
pact on water partitioning, consequently influencing rivers and

bodies of water (Zhang et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2022). Ad-
ditionally, climate plays a crucial role in shaping the design
of buildings, constructions, and roads, as they are influenced
by temperature and precipitation considerations (Golany, 1996,
Givoni, 1998). As a result, there is a substantial disparity in land-
cover characteristics among different climate zones, leading to
the climate domain gap.
Due to the climate domain gap, optimal land-cover semantic
segmentation models learned from a certain climate zone may
quickly lose their effectiveness on new images captured from
different climate zones. This problem significantly hampers
the practical application of land-cover semantic segmentation.
However, there is a lack of relevant research investigating the
impact of climate on land-cover semantic segmentation. To
enhance the model’s generalizability for land-cover semantic
segmentation, an appropriate climate domain adaptive dataset is
urgently needed.
In this paper, we explore the impact of climate on land-cover
semantic segmentation for the first time. To accomplish this,
we present a distinctive large-scale Climate-Aware Satellite Im-
ages Dataset (CASID) designed specifically for domain adaptive
land-cover semantic segmentation. CASID comprises image
samples with large image sizes from four representative cli-
mate zones: temperate monsoon (TemMs), subtropical monsoon
(SubMs), tropical monsoon (TroMs), and tropical rainforest
(TroRf), abbreviated for brevity. These climate zones form
four sub-datasets/domains based on their climate types, making
CASID the first land-cover semantic segmentation dataset to
incorporate multiple climate domains.
To enhance diversity and increase the challenge of our CASID,
we gathered 980 satellite images at a size of 5, 000 × 5, 000 from
30 different regions around Asia. These regions encompass
densely populated urban areas, sparsely populated suburbs, and
rural zones, covering a total area of over 24,500 square kilome-
ters. For each satellite image, we provide accurate pixel-wise
semantic segmentation labels for five categories: background,
building, forest, road, and water. The dataset production process
ensures the high diversity and complexity of CASID. Conse-
quently, as shown in Table 1, the widely-used semantic seg-
mentation method for domain adaptation, DeepLabV2 (Chen
et al., 2017), achieves only 39.9∼60.9 mIoU on different sub-
sets. When evaluated across climate zones on CASID, the per-
formance degrades catastrophically. For instance, the model
trained on the temperate monsoon climate zone achieves only
12.2 mIoU on the tropical rainforest climate zone. The cross-
domain results depicted in Figures 1(b), (c), and (e) visually
confirm these quantitative findings. These results demonstrate
that CASID can serves as a challenging benchmark for vali-
dating and developing robust methods in land-cover semantic
segmentation and unsupervised domain adaptation.
Besides, to shed light on the domain gap caused by climate,
we conducted an analysis of images from these four typical
climate zones. The analysis reveals large differences in global
image features, image texture, category distribution, spectral
value, and object shape between different climate zones. These
differences contribute to the domain gap and the degradation of
optimal models trained on certain climate zones. Furthermore,

2. Related works
In this section, we first describe the development of land-cover
datasets for semantic segmentation and then introduce semantic
segmentation methods.
Finally, we provide an overview of
unsupervised domain adaptation methods.
2.1. Land-Cover Datasets for Semantic Segmentation
Recently, with the advancement of deep learning technol-
ogy, large-scale datasets have played a crucial role in advancing
scene understanding tasks such as land-cover semantic segmen-
tation (Tong et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2021a). As shown in
Table 2, datasets such as ISPRS Vaihingen, ISPRS Potsdam,
Zurich Summer (Volpi and Ferrari, 2015), Zeebruges (Marcos
et al., 2018a), USGS SIRI-WHU (Zhong et al., 2015), EvLab-
SS (Zhang et al., 2017), and LandCover.ai (Boguszewski et al.,
2021) are collected for land-cover semantic segmentation. These
datasets provide remote sensing images with accurate pixel-wise
land-cover labels and have contributed to the development of
land-cover semantic segmentation methods. However, these
datasets cover relatively small areas and contain a limited num-
ber of annotated image samples, which limits the generalization
ability of relevant algorithms.
To address this limitation, several large-scale datasets have
been proposed, including SpaceNet (Van Etten et al., 2018),
DeepGlobe (Demir et al., 2018), AIRS (Chen et al., 2019), Land-
CoverNet (Alemohammad and Booth, 2020), GID dataset (Tong
et al., 2020), and LoveDA (Wang et al., 2021a). While these
datasets collect image samples from different cities, they do not
adequately consider the domain gap caused by the significant
diversity among different geographic areas.
Specifically, land cover can be influenced and diversified
by two factors: human management and climate (Wang et al.,
2021a, Dale, 1997, Reidmiller et al., 2019). LoveDA (Wang
et al., 2021a) demonstrates that human management contributes
to differentiation in the human living environment due to societal
and economic development, causing a domain gap between
urban and rural zones for land-cover semantic segmentation.
OpenEarthMap (Xia et al., 2023) collects samples from six
continents to construct a global benchmark dataset with 2.2
million segments of aerial and satellite samples with large image
sizes. However, the impact of climate on land-cover semantic
segmentation, which is more profound than human management,
has received limited investigation. The domain gap caused by
climate can significantly degrade the performance of semantic
segmentation methods, rendering them ineffective in practical
applications. To improve the model’s generalizability for large-
scale land-cover semantic segmentation, we have collected and
annotated a large-scale Climate-Aware Satellite Image Dataset
for land-cover semantic segmentation.
2.2. Semantic Segmentation
Land-cover semantic segmentation is a long-standing research
topic that assigns a label to each pixel, also known as pixel-level
classification. As a powerful technique for Earth observation, it
has been widely explored over the past few decades Existing
satellite image semantic segmentation methods are eminently
motivated by the fully-convolutional networks (FCNs) (Long
et al., 2015). Several earlier works (Kaiser et al., 2017, Mar-
manis et al., 2018, Marcos et al., 2018b) focus on using multi-
level semantic features on local patterns of images using deep
CNN. Besides, there are also some methods (Mou et al., 2019)
capturing long-range spatial relationships between entities by
proposing spatial and channel relation modules. Though previ-
ous works have achieved great advances, there are two critical
issues, one is an extremely foreground-background imbalanced
distribution, and the other is multiple small objects along with
the complex background. Recently, FarSeg (Zheng et al., 2020)
utilizes relation-based and optimization-based foreground mod-
eling to handle the foreground-background imbalance problems
in remote sensing imagery. Factseg (Ma et al., 2021) uses a fore-
ground activation object representation framework made up of a
dual-branch decoder and collaborative probability loss. It effec-
tively suppresses background distractions while also enhancing
the small object features.
2.3. Unsupervised Domain Adaptation
Unsupervised domain adaptation is a technique aimed at re-
ducing the performance loss caused by the domain gap.
It
involves transferring a model trained on the source domain to
the target domain. This technique is effective in reducing the
domain gap caused by climate. Recent advancements in unsu-
pervised domain adaptation have been made in two branches:
adversarial training and self-training.
Adversarial training methods typically involve an extractor
that learns domain-invariant features and a discriminator that
distinguishes these features. Tsai et al. (Tsai et al., 2018) utilize
spatial similarities in semantic outputs to adapt the structured
output space through adversarial learning. To address the mis-
alignment of category-level joint distribution, Luo et al. (Luo
et al., 2019) introduce a category-level adversarial network that
aligns each class with an adaptive adversarial loss. Wang et
al. (Wang et al., 2020b) align class-level features through a fine-
grained adversarial learning framework. To avoid distorting

Self-training methods generate pseudo-labels on the target
data and fine-tune the model on the source data alternately.
Lian et al. (Lian et al., 2019) connect the self-training and cur-

curriculum. It observes the target properties and fuses multi-

To solve the class imbalance problem of pseudo-labels in self-
training, Zou et al. (Zou et al., 2018) propose a class-balanced
self-training strategy to sample pseudo-labels. To improve the
quality of pseudo-labels and achieve sample balance, Mei et
al. (Mei et al., 2020) design an instance adaptive self-training
selector, which can involve more useful information for train-
ing. Recently, Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2021b) propose a novel
uncertainty-aware pseudo label refinery framework for utiliz-
ing both accurate prediction and uncertain prediction with low
confidence in pseudo labels. Cheng et al. (Cheng et al., 2021)
propose a novel dual-path learning framework to alleviate vi-
sual inconsistency. Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2020) leverage
co-occurring patterns between pairwise pixels in the output of
structured semantic segmentation. Yuan et al. (Yuan et al., 2022)
propose an Inter-class Separation and Intra-class Aggregation
(ISIA) mechanism to address the class confusion problem and
designs an Adaptive-weighted Instance Matching (AIM) strategy
for instance-level adaptation. Hoyer et al. (Hoyer et al., 2022)
propose DAFormer, which utilizes a Transformer encoder and
multi-level context-aware feature fusion decoder and achieves
state-of-the-art performance with significant improvement.

ance makes it more challenging, as demonstrated in Table 1,
while the high intra-domain variance benefits unsupervised do-
main adaptation, as discussed in Section 5.
Well annotated. For each satellite image, we provide accurate
pixel-wise semantic segmentation labels for five categories, i.e.,
background, building, forest, road, and water. They are anno-
tated by a group of experts and labeled with black, yellow, green,
red, and blue, as shown in Figure 2.
Complex background. The remote sensing land-cover seman-
tic segmentation task is always faced with complex background
samples (i.e., land-cover objects that are not of interest) (Pang
et al., 2019, Zheng et al., 2020).
The large image size and
diverse scenes in the CASID dataset provide rich details and in-
troduce a larger intra-class variance for the background samples.
This benefits the learning of semantic segmentation models and
helps them effectively handle the complex background in remote
sensing images.
Category imbalance.
Different land types naturally exhibit
diverse shapes and sizes. For instance, the road category is
characterized by its elongated and narrow form, while the for-
est category can span a significant surface area. This inherent
variation in shape and size contributes to the challenge of cat-
egory imbalance. As a result, the proportions of different land
categories differ significantly, further exacerbating the issue.
The presence of category imbalance poses a unique challenge
for tasks such as semantic segmentation and unsupervised do-
main adaptation. Addressing this challenge requires developing
effective strategies to handle the varying proportions of land
categories within the dataset.
3.2. Statistics Analysis for Climate Domain Gap
The climate greatly affects the manifestation of land cover. In
general, the climate is the major determinant of vegetation (Pren-
tice, 1990, Woodward et al., 2004, Salazar et al., 2007). Climate
zones with higher temperatures and more rainfall support more
productive and diverse plant communities, while regions with
lower temperatures and less precipitation typically have less
productive vegetation. The climate also impacts other land cover
types, such as water bodies, which are affected by precipitation
and evapotranspiration.
Additionally, the appearance of buildings and roads are also
affected by the climate, they are designed with considerations
of temperature and precipitation (Golany, 1996, Givoni, 1998).
For example, areas with high precipitation levels may require
the use of different building materials or construction methods
to prevent water damage. Areas with high temperatures may
require materials with higher thermal insulation to maintain
a comfortable indoor temperature. Similarly, roads may be
designed with different materials and widths based on the climate

4.3. Quantitative Results
The quantitative results are provided in Table 4. We can
see the categories of road and water are more challenging than
building and forest, due to their thin and long shapes. The
FCN8S (Long et al., 2015) and PAN (Li et al., 2018) with a
shallow backbone of VGG-16 (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2015)
and ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016) show relatively low perfor-
mance, i.e., 57.2 mIoU and 56.0 mIoU. By using multi-scale
context information, deeplabV3+ (Chen et al., 2018) and PSP-
Net (Zhao et al., 2017) achieve 56.9 mIoU and 57.1 mIoU.
FarSeg (Zheng et al., 2020) and FactSeg (Ma et al., 2021) im-
prove the semantic segmentation model to pay attention to the
foreground of remote sensing images, and their performance
reaches 58.2 mIoU and 58.0 mIoU. With a stronger backbone of
HRNet-W32 (Wang et al., 2020a), HRNet (Wang et al., 2020a)
achieves better performance, i.e., 60.9 mIoU, especially for
road categories. U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015), U-Net++,
and LinkNet (Chaurasia and Culurciello, 2017) enhance the se-
mantic segmentation model by utilizing detail information in
shallow features. Thus they achieve much better accuracy for
categories of road and water, which require detail information on
the most. Among them, the LinkNet (Chaurasia and Culurciello,
2017) achieves a high accuracy of 62.3 mIoU. By utilizing a
self-attention mechanism, recent transformer-based architectures
such as SegFormer (Xie et al., 2021) and PoolFormer (Yu et al.,
2022) have shown competitive results in land-cover semantic
segmentation, achieving 63.0 and 62.7 mIoU, respectively. In
contrast, the recent CNN-based SegNeXt (Guo et al., 2022)
employs efficient convolutional attention and has achieved the
highest results of 63.4 mIoU.

5. Experiments for Unsupervised Domain Adaptation
In this section, we evaluate the advanced Unsupervised Do-
main Adaptation (UDA) methods on the CASID dataset. We
choose two mainstream UDA approaches, i.e., adversarial train-
ing (AdaptSeg (Tsai et al., 2018), CLAN (Luo et al., 2019),
FADA (Wang et al., 2020b)) and self-training (CBST (Zou et al.,
2018), PyCDA (Lian et al., 2019)). Recent methods ASA (Zhou
et al., 2020) and DAFormer (Hoyer et al., 2022) are also included.
The evaluation uses the same metrics as that of the semantic seg-
mentation task (Long et al., 2015, Chen et al., 2018), i.e., mean
intersection over union (mIoU). We conduct all experiments five
times and present the mean and variance results.
5.1. Implementation details
All UDA methods adopt the same feature extractor and dis-
criminator, following the common practice in (Wang et al.,
2020b, Tsai et al., 2018).

5.2. Quantitative Results
Dataset comparison. We provide results reported on the two
most relevant existing datasets, i.e., LoveDA (Wang et al., 2021a)
and OpenEarthMap (Xia et al., 2023), and compare their cross-
domain performance gap with the proposed CASID datasets.
The cross-domain performance gap is the performance differ-
ence between cross-domain results (train on a source domain A
and test on a different target domain B) and target-only results
(train and test on the same domain). It demonstrates the perfor-
mance degradation caused by the domain gap in the datasets. A
higher cross-domain performance gap indicates a larger domain
gap between different domains in the dataset, which makes the
dataset more challenging. As shown in Tables 5, 6, and 7, cross-
domain performance gap in the LoveDA (Wang et al., 2021a)
and OpenEarthMap (Xia et al., 2023) are 14.4∼15.1 mIoU and
17.3∼25.6 mIoU, respectively.
In contrast, the cross-domain
performance gap in the proposed CASID dataset is 31.4∼51.5,
which is much higher than existing datasets. This demonstrates
that the proposed CASID dataset can serve as a more challenging
benchmark for both semantic segmentation and multi-domain
unsupervised domain adaptation tasks.
Moreover, in the existing datasets LoveDA (Wang et al.,
2021a) and OpenEarthMap (Xia et al., 2023), samples are simply
divided into different domains based on geographical location.
On the contrary, the proposed CASID is climate-aware and col-
lected from 30 areas in different climate zone, which is unique.
Notice that CASID can also be divided based on geographical
location if needed. The challenging and distinct characteristics
of the CASID dataset can be highly beneficial to the computer
vision and remote sensing community, and accelerate progress
in relevant research.

To eliminate any interference caused by differences in sam-

we uniformly select 25% and 50% samples in TemMs. We
refer to these uniformly sampled versions as TemMs-25%-U
and TemMs-50%-U, respectively. The protocol for TemMs-

TemMs-25/50%-U has exactly the same number of samples as

TemMs-25/50%. Noting that TemMs-25/50%-U have the same
number of samples for training with TemMs-25/50%, the only
main difference between TemMs-25/50%-U and TemMs-25/50%
is variance. Therefore, we can conclude that the reduction in
UDA performance from TemMs-25/50%-U to TemMs-25/50%

Instead, it is attributed to the lower intra-domain variance. Inter-

with similar variance, it achieves comparable results to TemMs-

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we investigate the impact of climate on land-
cover semantic segmentation for the first time. To this end,
we introduce a unique large-scale Climate-Aware Satellite Im-
ages Dataset (CASID) for domain adaptive land-cover semantic
segmentation.
It consists of 980 satellite images with a size
of 5, 000 × 5, 000 pixels, collected from 30 different regions
in four typical climate zones: temperate monsoon, subtropi-
cal monsoon, tropical monsoon, and tropical rainforest. This
makes our CASID the first land-cover semantic segmentation
dataset with multiple climate domains. It not only benefits the
research community in advancing domain adaptive land-cover
semantic segmentation with higher generalization ability but
also shows the potential to become a challenging benchmark
for the design of robust vision algorithms beyond the field of
remote sensing.
Moreover, we conduct a series of statistical
analyses on images collected from the four typical climate zones.
These analyses reveal significant differences in global image
features, image texture, category distribution, spectral values,
and object shapes among these zones, resulting in a climate
domain gap. These findings provide valuable insights to inspire
subsequent research in this field. Additionally, we evaluate var-
ious state-of-the-art semantic segmentation and unsupervised
domain adaptation methods using our CASID dataset. These
evaluations establish a solid baseline for future research. On the
basis of these experimental results, we conclude that CASID
can serve as a benchmark dataset for evaluating the performance
of land-cover semantic segmentation and unsupervised domain
adaptation methods. We hope that this work can benefit the
research community by advancing land-cover segmentation with
improved generalization ability and inspiring further research in
this field.
In the future, our aim is to explore and develop an unsuper-
vised domain adaptation method to address the domain gap
caused by climate, using the CASID dataset as a foundation.
Moreover, we plan to expand the CASID dataset to include more
types of climate zones and collect image samples from a global
perspective.
